\begin{abstract}
An image generator can respond strongly to a painter's name without recovering
what distinguishes that painter from others. We test this distinction using
Monet, Sisley, Pissarro and C\'ezanne, 649 digital painting reproductions, and
31 interpretable color, spatial and texture features. A prospective experiment
crosses six image models with 14 fixed scenes, a generic oil-painting clause,
four painter clauses and an artist-free control, with two repeated requests per
cell. Centering the named outputs within each scene removes their common
response. We then measure reference-aligned artist variation and use independent
repeats to correct the noise bias in artist-geometry error. Scene-paired
regressions compare models on the same reference target. Supporting palette and
processing interventions distinguish a shared painting response from additional
painter effects and expose measurement sensitivity. The primary collection is
in progress; its results and conclusions will be inserted after terminal
measurement under the frozen analysis specification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
``In the style of Monet'' is an instruction to distinguish one painter from
others. Yet a visibly painterly output need not satisfy that instruction. A
common change in color, contrast or surface texture can make images look less
photographic while leaving the differences between Monet, Sisley, Pissarro and
C\'ezanne poorly reproduced. Even a generator that produces distinguishable
outputs for all four names may distinguish them along the wrong visual axes.
The central question is therefore whether painter-name prompting recovers
\emph{artist-specific variation}, beyond the appearance shared by its named
outputs.

Distance to a painter's collection alone cannot settle this question. A generic
painting transformation could lower that distance for several painters at once.
Conversely, correct differences between painters could coexist with a common
offset from all reference paintings. These are distinct errors with different
implications for model comparison. A permutation of painter labels illustrates
the problem: generated collections can remain separable while their names map
to the wrong reference painters. An evaluation should separate the common
response, the amplitude of reference-aligned differences, and artist contrasts
that depart from the reference geometry.

We make that separation experimentally and statistically. Six generators receive
the same scene descriptions with either no painting clause, a generic oil-painting
clause, or one of four painter clauses. We subtract the mean response across
painters within each model and scene, then compare the remaining painter
contrasts with those in a fixed reference panel. Two repeated requests permit
cross-product estimation of conditional mean error without its usual additive
sampling-noise bias. A model cannot improve this endpoint merely by applying
the same painting-like change to every named output.

This design also gives a limited but testable answer to \emph{why} generated
collections differ. A shared painting shift, weak or exaggerated artist
contrasts, differences along other feature axes, and dependence on the depicted
scene produce different signatures in the decomposition. Generic-clause and
palette interventions probe alternatives to a uniquely artist-specific response.
These are explanations of observable output behavior. They do not identify
training frequencies, guidance rules or the internal representation of a painter.

The contribution is a controlled test of reference-relative artist specificity,
with a common target for comparing six current model configurations. It combines
painter contrasts, generic controls and repeat-corrected error in an interpretable
feature space. We retain distributional proximity and spread as complementary
outcomes: reproducing differences among painter means is a narrower achievement
than reproducing the painters' full distributions.

\section{Related work}
Artist imitation and computational style measurement are established research
problems. \citet{somepalli2024style} develop learned style descriptors and study
style similarity across diffusion models. \citet{su2025prompted} evaluate
prompted-artist recognition using content-controlled name substitutions; they
also compare named and artist-free images with real-artist prototypes in CLIP
space. Their work already establishes that artist names can leave recognizable
signatures. Our question is whether those signatures recover the \emph{relative
geometry of the reference painters}, after removing the shared response. Neither
name substitution nor cross-model comparison is claimed as a new method here.

\citet{deliege2025} examine stylistic imitation and stereotyping with expert
assessments of historical and generated art. AI-Pastiche studies authenticity
judgments and prompt adherence \citep{asperti2025pastiche}; AI-WikiArt uses
painting-derived captions and vision-language models to study attribution
\citep{fu2025aiwikiart}. These studies motivate distinguishing convincing
imitation, recognizable authorship and distributional recovery. We measure the
last of these in explicit digital feature coordinates, without substituting
those coordinates for expert judgments.

Quantitative art research has examined color, composition and image statistics
across painting collections \citep{kim2014,sigaki2018,lee2020}. \citet{kim2026}
combine formal and contextual representations to study art-historical change,
including contextual image-to-image experiments. Our intervention instead adds
painter names to text-only scene prompts, with no original composition supplied
to the generator. Related content and style remain entangled in our reference
panel; the common-scene intervention controls the requested content of generated
images, not the content of historical works.

Generative-model evaluation distinguishes fidelity and diversity
\citep{naeem2020}, and conditional metrics distinguish variation within conditions
from relationships among their means \citep{benny2021conditional}. Our
cross-repeat estimator follows the established principle of estimating squared
signal differences from independent partitions \citep{diedrichsen2017representational}.
Energy statistics provide the complementary distributional endpoint
\citep{szekely2013}. Processing can materially change image comparisons
\citep{parmar2022}; separation in a learned embedding is likewise not itself
perceptual validation \citep{asperti2026}. Together, these precedents motivate
a test of reference-relative artist contrasts with generic controls and
independent repeats. The contribution is the combined experimental test and
its findings, rather than a new style metric.

\section{Design and measurements}\label{sec:design}
\subsection{Painters and reference images}
The primary panel comprises 649 distinct painting reproductions: 297 Monet,
106 Sisley, 141 Pissarro and 105 C\'ezanne. Artist means receive equal weight,
so the larger Monet collection does not dominate the between-artist target.
A separate development panel of 221 works supplies feature scaling; no primary
reference or generated image is used to fit that transformation. Both panels
were assembled before the present experiment and had been used in earlier
analyses. They are explicit, finite comparison panels rather than newly withheld
samples of each painter's complete oeuvre.

Reference files are linked to recorded work identities and Wikimedia Commons
source metadata. Commons supports traceable file histories, source and rights
fields, and institutional collection contributions through its cultural-heritage
partnerships \citep{commonsglam}. Wikidata supplies structured, reusable work
and artist identities \citep{vrandecic2014wikidata}. These properties make the
selection and provenance inspectable; they do not guarantee uniform photography,
color calibration or conservation state. We therefore call the inputs
\emph{digital reproductions}, not direct measurements of physical paintings.
Previous computational art corpora support the use of documented reproductions
for comparative analysis, but do not authenticate this collection: the Web
Gallery of Art in \citet{kim2014}, ART500K in \citet{kim2026}, and WikiArt in
\citet{fu2025aiwikiart} are distinct from Wikimedia Commons.

\subsection{A common-scene, six-model experiment}
We compare GPT Image 1, GPT Image 2, GPT Image 2.5 Flare, GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst,
Nano Banana 2 and FLUX.2 Max. Fourteen authored outdoor scenes span water,
built, land and mixed compositions. Each scene is crossed with six clauses:
no painting instruction; ``Render as an oil painting''; and ``Render as an oil
painting in the style of [painter]'' for each of the four painters. The scene
text is otherwise identical. Each model--scene--clause cell receives two
separately requested outputs, giving 1,008 planned images.

All models receive text only and a square image request at approximately
1K resolution. The four OpenAI models use medium quality; the other two use
their specified 1K configurations. These comparisons concern the recorded
configurations, not maximum attainable quality or equal expenditure per image.
The exact prompts, parameters and randomized request order were fixed before
collection. The 14 scenes are a fixed design,
not a random sample of all possible content.

\subsection{What the features measure}\label{sec:features}
Images undergo orientation correction and conversion to sRGB using valid
embedded profiles; compatible unprofiled files are treated as sRGB. The primary
pipeline preserves aspect ratio at a 512-pixel short side, using Lanczos resizing
without upsampling. Each of the 31 coordinates is centered and scaled by its
median and interquartile range in the development panel, with equal total
weight per painter. Thus a unit is a development-panel IQR in that coordinate,
not a perceptual just-noticeable difference.

Table~\ref{tab:features} explains the representation. Color features summarize
lightness and chroma, concentration of hue, and color changes across spatial
scales; CIEDE2000 supplies the color-difference calculation
\citep{sharma2005ciede}. Spatial features describe how luminance contrast is
organized across frequencies, directions and regions. Texture features summarize
multiscale detail and local intensity patterns using wavelets and local binary
patterns \citep{mallat1989wavelet,ojala2002lbp}. For example, similar mean chroma
can coexist with different hue diversity, and similar total contrast can coexist
with different edge orientation or fine-scale texture. These distinctions make
family-specific results interpretable, while correlated coordinates and
processing sensitivity prevent treating the Euclidean metric as a validated
measure of artistic style.

\section{Separating common appearance from artist recovery}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Reference-aligned variation}
Let $\mu_a\in\R^{31}$ be the standardized reference mean for painter $a$ and
let $\bar\mu=\frac14\sum_a\mu_a$. Define the reference painter contrasts
$r_a=\mu_a-\bar\mu$ and their total squared magnitude $H=\sum_a\|r_a\|^2$.
For generated vector $z_{msak}$ from model $m$, scene $s$, painter $a$ and
repeat $k\in\{1,2\}$, remove the common named response:
\begin{equation}
 d_{msak}=z_{msak}-\frac14\sum_b z_{msbk}.
 \label{eq:center}
\end{equation}
Any shift shared by all four named outputs of that model, scene and repeat
cancels exactly. This includes a common painting effect and any model-specific
baseline that acts additively in these coordinates.

The reference-aligned slope is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\beta_{msk}=\frac{\sum_a\langle d_{msak},r_a\rangle}{H},\qquad
 \widehat\beta_m=\frac1S\sum_s\frac{\widehat\beta_{ms1}+\widehat\beta_{ms2}}2.
 \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
It is the least-squares coefficient of generated painter contrasts on reference
contrasts. A value of zero indicates no response along the reference direction;
one matches its amplitude. Values above one indicate exaggeration along that
direction, not necessarily better recovery. A nonzero slope also does not
exclude substantial differences along other axes.

\subsection{Noise-corrected error and its explanation}
Two independently requested repeats permit the cross-product estimator
\begin{equation}
 \widehat D_{ms}=\frac1H\sum_a
 \langle d_{msa1}-r_a,\ d_{msa2}-r_a\rangle,\qquad
 \widehat D_m=\frac1S\sum_s\widehat D_{ms}.
 \label{eq:distortion}
\end{equation}
Under stable conditional means and independent, mean-zero repeat errors, its
expectation is the squared error of the scene-conditional mean painter
contrasts, normalized by $H$. The error contributed by output sampling does not
inflate this expectation. Exact mean-geometry recovery gives expected $D=0$;
a purely common named response gives expected $D=1$. Finite estimates may be
negative. We do not truncate them or interpret a negative estimate as
better-than-perfect recovery.

The estimator has an exact decomposition. Write
$e_{msak}=d_{msak}-\widehat\beta_{msk}r_a$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \widehat D_{ms}=
 (\widehat\beta_{ms1}-1)(\widehat\beta_{ms2}-1)
 +\frac1H\sum_a\langle e_{msa1},e_{msa2}\rangle.
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is error in the amplitude of the reference-aligned response;
the second is error orthogonal to that direction. This distinguishes weak or
exaggerated conditioning from a different organization of painter contrasts.
Both terms retain the cross-repeat correction and can be negative in finite
samples. Computing the same error after averaging over scenes supplies a
separate descriptive target: low aggregate error can conceal scene-dependent
failures.

\subsection{Comparing models}
We fit the scene fixed-effects regression
\begin{equation}
 \widehat D_{ms}=\alpha_s+\gamma_m+\varepsilon_{ms},
 \qquad\gamma_{\text{GPT Image 2}}=0.
 \label{eq:regression}
\end{equation}
All models face the same scenes and equally weighted reference painter target.
With a complete balanced panel, model coefficient differences equal the mean
paired scene differences. Inference uses those scene differences rather than
treating features, painters or individual images as independent replicates.

The fixed inferential family contains six reference-aligned slopes and 15
pairwise model error contrasts. We report paired-scene Student intervals with
$n-1$ degrees of freedom and Bonferroni adjustment across these 21 comparisons.
Nominal 95\% intervals for absolute $D_m$ are descriptive. These are approximate
summaries of variation across the fixed briefs under the assumed error model;
randomized request order does not establish independence or justify inference
to arbitrary scenes. A minimum of 12 complete paired scenes is required.
Missing comparisons, their available scenes and the panel complete for all
models are reported explicitly. No incomplete scene is repaired with an image
from another collection.

Prespecified sensitivity analyses remove each scene in turn, resample scenes
for model contrasts, and resample reference works within painter while holding
generated vectors fixed. The last analysis assesses dependence on this reference
panel; it is not a second independent uncertainty estimate. We also repeat the
analysis in each feature family, without texture, and using central square
measurement windows for both generated and reference images. The square view
controls measurement-window aspect ratio while discarding peripheral content.
These analyses are descriptive and do not select a preferred representation.

A separately declared sensitivity addresses unequal subject mixtures in the
references. Before inspecting the new feature outcomes, we specified equal
weight for four previously recorded content classes within each painter, then
recomputed the reference contrasts and all model estimates. For example, water
scenes are much more frequent in the Monet panel than in the Pissarro panel.
Equal class weighting tests dependence on that broad mixture, without matching
individual compositions or identifying pure style. Reference resampling within
artist/class strata assesses the stability of this alternative target.

\subsection{Generic controls and full distributions}
The mean change from the free arm to the four named arms separates into a common
component and centered painter contrasts. We compare the direction and magnitude
of the common component with the free-to-generic change. Their cosine similarity
and cross-repeat squared magnitudes test whether a generic painting clause
reproduces the shared direction. A large common fraction alone cannot show that
naming fails: these four reference painters also share visual properties.
Equations~\ref{eq:beta}--\ref{eq:distortion} supply the separate recovery test.

For painter $a$, energy discrepancy compares its generated collection $Y_a$
with reference collection $X_a$:
\begin{equation}
 \E(X,Y)=\frac{2}{n_Xn_Y}\sum_{i,j}\|x_i-y_j\|
 -\frac1{n_X^2}\sum_{i,i'}\|x_i-x_{i'}\|
 -\frac1{n_Y^2}\sum_{j,j'}\|y_j-y_{j'}\|.
 \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Unlike distance to a mean, this statistic incorporates within-collection
variation. Its empirical baseline need not be zero for two independent samples
from the same distribution. We also report generated/reference total-variance
ratios. These complementary outcomes can reveal narrow or displaced generated
distributions even when reference painter contrasts are recovered.

\section{What the supporting interventions explain}\label{sec:why}
\subsection{Proximity gains do not establish painter specificity}
An earlier exploration used the same four-painter reference panel with 1,536
named and 384 artist-free outputs from GPT Image 1 and GPT Image 2 request groups,
three prompt methods and 16 scenes. Across its 24 painter--model--method cells,
generated/reference trace ratios ranged from .206 to .376. Generated and
reference images were also separable by a grouped classifier, with balanced
accuracy .940--.983. Figure~\ref{fig:four} shows the corresponding projected
distributions. These findings motivated the present experiment; they do not
supply its inferential evidence or establish perceptual differences.

The effect of naming on reference proximity was not uniform. In matched-size
real/real comparisons, generated/reference energy exceeded the real/real median
for every painter. Pooling the explored named conditions lowered the energy
median relative to the free control only for Sisley. A later controlled
Monet/C\'ezanne panel, with 24 shared scenes and three repeats, produced negative
named-minus-free energy changes in all six model--painter comparisons; four
rejected in their original eight-test family. The different reference panels,
prompts and collection conditions preclude treating these as a single ranking.

A separate prospective C\'ezanne experiment supplied the stronger generic
control: 96 outputs on 24 new scenes, with two repeats per clause. Naming
reduced energy relative to the generic painting clause by 1.195
($p=.00001$, fixed $\alpha=.025$), while the named/generic trace ratio was .552.
Thus a generic clause did not reproduce the entire proximity gain in that
comparison. This is compatible with either correct or incorrect painter-specific
contrasts; the current four-painter design directly distinguishes those cases.

\subsection{Generic painting can alter responsiveness}
Two separate palette experiments crossed six scenes with muted/vivid
instructions and free, generic, Monet and C\'ezanne clauses, producing 192
outputs per collection. Their primary endpoint was the painter-minus-generic
difference in the vivid-minus-muted response of median CIELAB chroma. Neither
collection resolved an additional painter-name interaction: the original
Monet and C\'ezanne estimates were $-.284$ and $-.018$ in development-IQR units,
with simultaneous intervals $[-.585,.016]$ and $[-.343,.308]$; the later estimates
were $-.159$ and $.037$, also with intervals spanning zero.

In contrast, the secondary generic-minus-free response was negative in both
collections: $-.853$ and $-.891$, with nominal 95\% intervals excluding zero.
A generic painting clause can therefore attenuate the measured palette response
without an identified additional name effect. This supports including a generic
control rather than attributing every named/free difference to painter
specificity. The unresolved interactions do not prove equivalence, and two
palette levels cannot distinguish reduced response gain from a shifted operating
range or saturation.

\subsection{Simple transformations explain some effects, but do not transfer uniformly}
Generated-only moment maps provide another proxy explanation. Translation
matches the mean of named outputs; translation plus isotropic scaling also
matches their total variance. In the earlier controlled panel, actual naming
had lower held-scene reference energy than the translation/scaling map in all
six model--painter averages. Mean and scalar spread changes therefore did not
exhaust the observed naming effect. Adding scale improved prediction of named
scene means in all six cells while worsening reference energy in five, showing
that these two evaluation targets can disagree.

That historical ordering did not transfer to a prospective 240-image
FLUX/C\'ezanne comparison on 12 new scenes. The unchanged translation/scaling
map improved both reference energy and repeat-corrected scene-mean prediction
relative to translation: differences $-.390$ and $-5.047$, with approximate
simultaneous intervals $[-.509,-.271]$ and $[-7.880,-2.214]$. The new scenes,
collection time and finite-sample energy target differed. This contrary result
rules out presenting the earlier ordering as a general mechanism. It motivates
the present scene-conditional artist-contrast analysis, which evaluates recovery
directly rather than identifying naming with a universal global transformation.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The distinction between a common painting response and recovery of painter
contrasts changes what counts as a successful model. Strong named/free movement
is insufficient, as is recognition of the requested name. The relevant test is
whether the differences generated by changing the name correspond to the
differences in the reference artists. The reference-aligned slope and corrected
error provide complementary answers: response amplitude alone cannot establish
correct geometry, and mean geometry alone cannot establish full-distribution
recovery.

The new experiment's model-specific interpretation will be completed after
terminal collection. Its decomposition will attribute observable error to
reference-aligned amplitude, other artist-contrast directions and scene
variation. These components are statistical explanations, not separate latent
modules in a generator. Training imbalance, associations between artists and
subjects, preference optimization and inference-time guidance remain possible
upstream causes; the present interventions do not distinguish them.

\subsection{Scope of the evidence}
Reference contrasts combine stylistic and content differences in this finite
panel. Holding generated scene text fixed controls the prompt intervention but
does not make historical content identical or verify that outputs follow every
scene instruction. Results therefore concern recovery of measured reference
painter differences under these outdoor briefs. They do not measure the complete
style of an artist or establish generalization to unrelated artistic traditions.
Four related painters make the common-response question useful but narrow its
external scope.

The representation is transparent but not perceptually calibrated. A separate
processing challenge found exact invariance for lossless round trips, while
resizing and blur materially changed texture coordinates. Eight-neighbor
local-binary-pattern entropy accounted for 83.9\% of squared texture displacement
under the tested blur. Common-square remeasurement retained all eight historical
energy-contrast signs and the four adjusted rejections, but controls only one
source of image geometry variation. Unknown photographic and reproduction
processes remain potential contributors to generated/reference differences.
No qualified independent-capture pairs or human style ratings are available.

Model comparisons apply to the requested configurations and collection period.
Their repeat correction requires stable conditional responses and independent
errors; randomized dispatch does not prove those conditions. The experiment has
14 scene clusters, so uncertainty may remain substantial despite many images.
Reference-work resampling and scene deletions assess sensitivity without
creating new independent replication. All collections were conducted by the
same research team; there is no separate-investigator replication.

\section{Conclusion}
Painter-name prompting should be evaluated by what distinguishes the reference
painters, alongside proximity to their full distributions. Common appearance,
reference-aligned variation and other artist contrasts are separable targets.
The present experiment applies this distinction to six generators with generic
controls, repeated common scenes and interpretable measurements. Final empirical
conclusions remain pending the frozen experiment's terminal analysis.

\section{Feature definitions}\label{app:features}
Color coordinates use CIELAB under D65 and the $2^\circ$ observer. Lightness
and chroma each contribute a median and IQR. Chromatic fraction is the share
of pixels with $C^*\geq5$. Hue concentration is the magnitude of the
chroma-weighted circular mean; hue entropy uses 24 equally spaced bins and is
normalized by their log count. Both use chroma weights and are zero when fewer
than 1\% of pixels are chromatic. At each of three lags (1\%, 4\% and 16\% of
the short side), the median is taken over pooled horizontal and vertical
CIEDE2000 differences. A log--log slope summarizes their
scale dependence \citep{sharma2005ciede}.

Spatial and texture coordinates use linear-sRGB luminance. A Tukey window
(parameter .1) precedes Fourier analysis. Thirty-two logarithmic radial bins
cover 4--128 cycles per short side; a Theil--Sen line gives spectral slope and
its root-mean-square residual. Power-weighted axial concentration measures
anisotropy. Scharr derivatives supply gradient median and IQR, an 18-bin
magnitude-weighted orientation entropy, and horizontal--vertical balance. Mean
Jensen--Shannon divergence of quadrant orientation histograms from the global
histogram measures regional variation.

Four log mean-squared detail energies come from a four-level undecimated
\texttt{db2} wavelet transform, followed by their linear slope and mean second
difference \citep{mallat1989wavelet}. Uniform local-binary-pattern entropy uses
$(P,R)=(8,1),(16,2),(32,4)$ on quantized luminance
\citep{ojala2002lbp}. Three median local coefficients of variation use window
widths obtained by rounding 1\%, 4\% and 16\% of the short side and increasing
even widths to the next odd integer, with a $10^{-6}$ mean floor.
The fixed implementation records boundary handling and stabilizing
constants. The separate scaler contains 101 Monet, 36 Sisley, 48 Pissarro and
36 C\'ezanne works. Equal painter mass is used to calculate each weighted
median and IQR.

\section{Estimator details and sensitivity analyses}\label{app:estimation}
Let $\theta_{msa}=\mathbb E[d_{msak}]$ and
$d_{msak}=\theta_{msa}+\eta_{msak}$. If repeat errors have zero mean and
$\mathbb E\langle\eta_{msa1},\eta_{msa2}\rangle=0$, then
\[
 \mathbb E[\widehat D_{ms}]
 =H^{-1}\sum_a\|\theta_{msa}-r_a\|^2.
\]
Centering induces dependence between artists within a repeat, but independent
repeat blocks preserve the vanishing cross-repeat term. A common response has
$\theta_{msa}=0$ and hence expected $D=1$, even if it shifts the entire named
collection toward the references. A correctly aligned but rescaled response
$\theta_{msa}=b r_a$ gives expected $D=(b-1)^2$. Orthogonal deviations add their
squared magnitude. This identity explains why more visibly distinct painter
outputs need not have lower recovery error.

For each model, global distortion applies the same cross-product estimator to
the scene-averaged contrasts. Its target differs from the mean scene-conditional
error: averaging can cancel scene-dependent deviations. Both are reported,
without selecting the smaller as the primary result. The primary comparison
weights scenes and artists equally, irrespective of the reference count.

For a model contrast, compute one error difference per complete paired scene.
Its standard error is the sample standard deviation of those differences divided
by $\sqrt n$. The simultaneous half-width is
$t_{n-1,1-.05/(2\cdot21)}\,\mathrm{SE}$. Six slope intervals use the same
family adjustment. Additional nominal intervals and 5,000 paired-scene bootstrap
draws are descriptive checks, not alternative routes to significance. Reference
sensitivity draws 1,000 bootstrap samples independently within each painter,
recomputes reference means and $H$, and holds generated vectors and scaling
fixed. It describes reference-panel dependence rather than all uncertainty
jointly.

The shared-effect diagnostic first averages each arm over scenes within repeat.
Let $h_{ak}$ be the named-minus-free difference and
$c_k=\frac14\sum_a h_{ak}$. The cross-repeat common and artist-specific squared
magnitudes are $4\langle c_1,c_2\rangle$ and
$\sum_a\langle h_{a1}-c_1,h_{a2}-c_2\rangle$. Their ratio is reported only
when the summed denominator is positive; finite corrected components can be
negative, so the ratio is not a probability. Generic/common cosine uses their
repeat-averaged vectors. This descriptive global statistic is separate from
the conditional recovery endpoint.

The four-painter exploration used repeated scenes and three prompt methods.
Its projected distributions, trace ratios and grouped discrimination are
post-result descriptions. The pooled named energy medians for Monet, Sisley,
Pissarro and C\'ezanne were 2.068, 1.678, 1.975 and 2.142, compared with free
medians 1.732, 1.878, 1.854 and 1.657. Matched-size real/real medians were
.193, .215, .164 and .199. These summaries neither isolate a prompt method
nor identify a generator mechanism.

The later controlled naming panel selected Monet and C\'ezanne after the
exploration. It crossed 24 scenes with three models, two clauses and three
repeats; 142 additional short-scene outputs supplied two prompt-length
comparisons. Reference panels contain 38 Monet and 32 C\'ezanne works.
Their broad content classes were used for prespecified weighting; class coding
was performed with an LLM by the study maintainer, without independent human
adjudication. That weighting is not used in the new experiment's equally
weighted artist-contrast endpoint.

The six controlled named-minus-free energy changes were $-.846,-1.818$ for
Nano Banana 2, $-.625,-.896$ for FLUX.2 Max, and $-.234,-.056$ for GPT Image 2
(Monet, C\'ezanne). The first four rejected after adjustment in the original
family of eight; the two GPT Image 2 contrasts did not. A separate 72-output
FLUX follow-up repeated the directions with changes $-.695,-.952$ and
adjusted rejection in its new four-endpoint family. These are repeated
collections by the same investigators, not independent-investigator replication.

Conditional variance and scene retrieval need not move together. In the
controlled FLUX/Monet panel, naming reduced between-scene spread but increased
held-repetition scene retrieval from 44.4\% to 59.7\%. Retrieval assigns each
held-out image to the nearest centroid formed from the other repetitions; it
measures relative feature distinguishability, not verified scene adherence.
This example prevents equating contraction with loss of all content response.

The palette experiments used six fixed scenes and four repeats per
arm/polarity. Original approximate intervals used a two-interaction family;
the later family also included two FLUX naming endpoints. Their unresolved
name-minus-generic estimates do not establish equality between names and a
generic clause. Secondary generic-minus-free intervals were nominal.

Historical transformation maps were fitted to generated outputs without
reference optimization and evaluated on held-out whole scenes. The prospective
fixed-map experiment used the original full fits unchanged, 12 new scenes and
ten repetitions. Its energy target depends on that repeat count; conditional
prediction error uses a repeat-noise correction. The two simultaneous
jackknife intervals assume stable independent repeat-block errors. The observed
conditional-error half-width exceeded its finite-proxy planning criterion,
so planning calculations did not guarantee achieved precision. The contrary
prospective result is retained without extra sampling.

\section{Collection provenance and computational scope}\label{app:provenance}
Historical GPT Image 1 and GPT Image 2 labels identify the requested model
names. A later technical negative control showed that the earlier image
interface also accepted an invented image-model identifier. This does not show
that valid names select the same underlying model, but successful request
acceptance alone cannot authenticate their separation. Historical contrasts
are therefore interpreted as recorded request-group comparisons. The current
experiment uses explicit documented model routes and fixed provider restrictions;
it does not reuse historical images as verified examples of the new variants.
Raw responses retain returned size, quality and model fields when available.
Underlying checkpoints and training corpora are not available for inspection.

The present experiment excludes technical probes and an earlier attempt closed
before feature measurement because model selection could not be qualified.
No image is shared between these collections. The earlier incomplete
generic-clause cohort is likewise not pooled with its complete 96-image
successor. Exact assignments, attempt outcomes and measurement exclusions are
retained in the versioned manifests; selection was not based on visual success.
